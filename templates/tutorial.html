    How reaction rules work

    A reaction rule describes one type of reaction as a change of a small graph: atoms are the nodes, bonds are the edges. LAGER uses the graph-grammar software MØD, and rules are written in its GML format. A rule has three parts:

    • left: bonds (and atoms) that exist only before the reaction, so they are broken.
    • right: bonds that exist only after the reaction, so they are formed.
    • context: atoms and bonds that must be present but do not change.

    In the rule pictures these are the panels L, K (context) and R. Broken bonds are red, formed bonds green. Every atom has an id (the small number); the same id is the same atom on both sides. Hydrogens are atoms of their own, so a rule that moves an H has to list it. A label is an element symbol, with a charge if needed (O-, N+), or a wildcard starting with _ (e.g. _X) that matches any atom.

    Two directions. The search works backwards, so it uses the retrosynthetic rule (inv_rules/<name>_inv.gml): its left side is the product, its right side the precursors. The forward rule (forward_rules/<name>.gml) is the same reaction written forwards. After each step LAGER applies all forward rules to the precursors to find side reactions with other groups of the molecule. When you add a rule (tab 3) you write the forward reaction, and LAGER creates the retrosynthetic rule from it.

    Where a rule matches. A rule can be applied wherever its left side (left + context) is found in a molecule. Atoms that are not in the rule are not checked: an atom may have more neighbours than the rule shows. A small rule is therefore very general, often too general. Two things make it more specific:

    • More context: more atoms around the reaction centre that must be present.
    • Conditions, which are checked on the left side. constrainAdj counts the neighbours of an atom, optionally only those with certain labels and bond types: constrainAdj [ id 1 op "=" count 2 nodeLabels [ label "O" ] ] means “atom 1 has exactly 2 O neighbours” (op can be =, <, <=, >, >=). constrainLabelAny lists what a wildcard may be: constrainLabelAny [ label "_X" labels [ label "Cl" label "Br" ] ].

    The three examples below show the reaction centre, the context and the conditions of real LAGER rules. The pictures show the forward reaction; the numbers are the atom ids of the rule. Click Show the rule to see the retrosynthetic rule as the search uses it.

    Rule names matter: names containing protection or deprotection are treated as cheap helper steps in the search.

    {{ example_head(1, "Amide coupling", "amide_coupling_inv", ["[2H:6][O:2][C:1](=[O:5])c1ccccc1", "[H:4][NH:3][CH2:7]c1ccccc1"], ["[O:5]=[C:1](c1ccccc1)[NH:3][CH2:7]c1ccccc1", "[H:4][O:2][2H:6]"], "1,2,3,4") }}

    Reaction centre

    Four bonds change. In the retrosynthetic direction the amide bond C1–N3 and the bond O2–H4 break, and C1–O2 and N3–H4 form: the amide becomes the carboxylic acid and the amine. Forwards this is acid + amine → amide + water.

    Atom 6 is labelled 2H: a marker for an activated acid (C(=O)O[2H], standing for the acid activated by a coupling reagent). The water-like molecule [2H]O (“activator”) is in the side-product list, so the search always has it. After this rule the search must apply COOH_activation next, which turns the marked acid back into the normal acid. In this way an amide coupling always comes with its activation step.

    Why the marker? Without it, the rule would say that any carboxylic acid and any amine react whenever they meet. LAGER would then expect, for example, an amino acid to react with every other acid in the flask, and even with itself, and the side-reaction check would report such reactions almost everywhere. In reality an amide coupling needs an activating (coupling) reagent. LAGER does not name a particular one, because the choice of activators is wide (carbodiimides, uronium salts, acid chlorides, …). Instead, the 2H atom is a general placeholder for “some activator”, which keeps the rule general.

    Context

    O5 (the C=O oxygen on C1) makes sure that only amides are cut, not every C–N bond. C7 requires a carbon on the nitrogen, so the amine is a primary or secondary amine, not ammonia. 2H6 on O2 is the activation marker described above.

    Conditions

    The retrosynthetic rule has three conditions:

    • C1 has exactly 1 O neighbour: only the C=O. This excludes carbamates (N–C(=O)–O), which are made differently. In the forward rule the same condition reads “exactly 2 O neighbours” (a carboxylic acid), because the rule itself adds the O–C1 bond. LAGER translates such counts automatically when it turns a rule around.
    • N3 has no S neighbour: no sulfonamides.
    • C7 has no double bond to O: the other substituent on N is not a carbonyl, so imides and ureas are not cut here.
    {{ example_head(2, "Grignard reaction", "Grignard_keto_aldeh_inv", ["[H:1][C:2](=[O:3])[c:7]1ccccc1", "C[CH2:6][Mg:5][Br:4]", "[H:8][O:9][H:10]"], ["[H:1][C:2]([O:3][H:8])([CH2:6]C)[c:7]1ccccc1", "[H:10][O:9][Mg:5][Br:4]"], "2,3,5,6,8,9") }}

    Reaction centre

    This rule includes the aqueous work-up. Forwards, the Grignard carbon C6 bonds to the carbonyl carbon C2, the C2=O3 double bond becomes single, and water protonates the alkoxide: H8 moves from O9 to O3, and O9 binds to Mg (HO–Mg–X). In the retrosynthetic direction the alcohol C2–O3–H8 is cut into the carbonyl compound, the Grignard reagent C6–Mg5–X4 and water.

    Context

    _Z1 and _Y7 are the other two substituents of the carbonyl carbon. They are wildcards that may be H or C, so one rule covers formaldehyde, aldehydes and ketones but not esters or amides (an O or N there would not match). _X4 is the halogen of the Grignard reagent (Cl, Br or I), and H10 belongs to the water of the work-up.

    Conditions

    • C6 has no double or triple bond to O, N or S: the carbon that carries Mg cannot be part of a C=O, C=N, C≡N or C=S group, because a Grignard reagent would attack it itself.
    • constrainLabelAny restricts the three wildcards: _X to Cl, Br, I and _Y, _Z to H, C.

    “Create rule from example reactions” makes wildcards like these automatically when your examples differ at an atom (e.g. one Grignard with Br, one with Cl).

    {{ example_head(3, "Michaelis–Arbuzov reaction", "Arbuzov_dialkylalkylphosphonites_Br_inv", ["C[CH2:13][O:12][P:9]([O:10][CH2:11]C)[c:14]1ccccc1", "[Br:3][CH2:1]c1ccccc1"], ["C[CH2:13][O:12][P:9](=[O:10])([CH2:1]c1ccccc1)[c:14]1ccccc1", "C[CH2:11][Br:3]"], "1,3,9,10,11") }}

    Reaction centre

    Forwards, the phosphorus P9 of the phosphonite attacks the alkyl bromide: the bond C1–Br3 breaks and P9–C1 forms. The bromide then removes an ethyl group from the P–O–ethyl: O10–C11 breaks, C11–Br3 forms (ethyl bromide), and O10 becomes the P=O oxygen. The retrosynthetic rule does the reverse: phosphinate + ethyl bromide → phosphonite + alkyl bromide.

    Context

    The context fixes the type of phosphorus compound. Besides O10, the phosphorus must have a second alkoxy group (O12–C13) and one carbon substituent (C14): a dialkyl alkyl/aryl phosphonite R–P(OR′)2. The other Arbuzov rules have the same reaction centre but a different context: three alkoxy groups (trialkyl phosphite) or one alkoxy group and two carbons (alkyl dialkylphosphinite).

    Conditions

    • C1 has no aromatic, double or triple bond: the halide is an sp³ alkyl halide. Both steps are SN2 reactions, which do not work on aryl or vinyl halides.
    • C11 has no aromatic, double or triple bond and at most 1 C neighbour: the group removed from oxygen is methyl or a primary alkyl (e.g. ethyl), which bromide can attack in an SN2 reaction.
